Leem Lab proposal · P01

Using Controlled In-Plane Lattice Strain to Tune Charge Transfer Between Monolayer MoS2 and Adsorbed Dyes

A Raman-compatible bending stage for reproducible strain on CVD-grown MoS2

Musa Ibne Mannan, Ph.D. candidate
Leem Research Group, Department of Mechanical Engineering, The University of Texas at Dallas
Prepared for Prof. Juyoung Leem · 29 October 2026 · Draft for discussion

1.Summary

Charge-transfer (CT) Raman enhancement on 2D semiconductors depends on how the energy levels of an adsorbed molecule line up with the band edges of the 2D layer. Researchers have tuned that alignment with pressure, gate voltage, chemical doping, alloy composition and layer thickness. Each of these either changes the sample, compresses the molecule along with the substrate, or needs electrodes. Controlled in-plane lattice strain, which moves the MoS2 band edges while the dye layer stays untouched, has not been tested as a CT control in any experiment we found.

We propose to build a four-point bending stage that fits under the lab Raman microscope, apply set, repeatable strain to monolayer MoS2 carrying a dye layer, and record the dye Raman signal, the MoS2 Raman modes and the MoS2 photoluminescence at the same spot at every step. The central question is whether dye CT enhancement on MoS2 follows the lattice strain in a reversible, dye-specific way once doping is accounted for. The outputs are a calibrated strain device, strain-response curves for two dyes at two lasers, a protocol that separates strain from doping, and a first manuscript.

2.Background

2.1Charge-transfer enhancement on a flat 2D surface

A flat 2D crystal has no plasmonic hot spots, so most of the Raman gain for a molecule sitting on it comes from the chemical mechanism. An electron moves between a molecular orbital and a band edge of the 2D layer when the laser energy matches that gap (Fig. 1). The closer the system is to that resonance, the larger the gain. Several papers show this directly:

  • Plain MoS2 is the weakest of graphene, h-BN and MoS2 for CuPc enhancement (Ling et al., 2014), which leaves room for a knob that improves it.
  • R6G enhancement on graphene, MoS2 and WSe2 tracks the photocurrent measured between R6G and the 2D layer (Lee et al., 2016).
  • In WS2(1−x)Se2x alloys the R6G 612 cm−1 band rises to a maximum at x = 0.46 and then falls, with an enhancement factor near 1.27 × 106 at the peak (Tang et al., 2023, Fig. 2b). Moving the band edges moved the CT resonance.
  • In a diamond anvil cell, R6G on MoS2/Au showed a maximum degree of CT (ρCT) at 2.39 GPa (Sun et al., 2019, Fig. 5g), and pressure switched monolayer MoS2 from resonance-dominated to CT-dominated enhancement (Sun et al., 2021).

2.2What strain does to MoS2

Tensile strain narrows the optical gap of monolayer MoS2. Frisenda et al. (2017) cite about 50 meV per % for uniaxial strain and about 100 meV per % for biaxial strain, and their Table 1 gives a measured A-exciton gauge of −51.1 meV per % for MoS2 on polypropylene and −12.5 meV per % on PDMS. That difference shows how much the substrate controls strain transfer. Under uniaxial strain the MoS2 E12g mode splits and red-shifts, by about 4.9 and 1.1 cm−1 per % for the two branches in free-standing MoS2 (Hwang and Suh, 2017). This gives an in-situ strain readout on the flake itself.

2.3Strain that our CVD films already carry

MoS2 grown on SiO2/Si carries about 0.4% biaxial tensile strain and the highest electron density of four substrates tested (Chae et al., 2017, Fig. 3a). Growth temperature changes it (Kataria et al., 2017). Within one flake the E12g position changes from perimeter to centre while A1g stays uniform, which points to a strain gradient without a matching doping gradient (Luo et al., 2020, Fig. 2c–d). Transfer polymers add strain and doping of their own: PMMA residue gave 0.07% tensile strain with p-doping and polystyrene gave 0.17% compressive strain with n-doping (Bhuyan et al., 2026).

3.Research gap

Fig. 2 summarizes how dye/2D charge transfer has been tuned so far, scored against what a clean test of the substrate’s band position needs: the same flake measured repeatedly, the dye left uncompressed, and an effect that acts on CT itself.

  • Pressure compresses the dye and the substrate together, so the shift in the dye’s own orbitals cannot be separated from the substrate’s (Sun et al., 2019; 2021). The same holds for 2D/2D charge transfer under pressure (Kim et al., 2022).
  • Alloys, doping and layer number change the sample between data points, which also changes defects and adsorbate coverage (Tang et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2016).
  • Gate voltage tunes CT in place but needs a device with electrodes and showed hysteresis in air (Xu et al., 2011).
  • Strain-tuned SERS on MoS2 exists only through plasmonic gaps: bending a flexible nanotip array changed the enhancement factor of the MoS2 2LA mode from 1.23 to 8.72 by changing gap width (Kim et al., 2024). A dye was not involved.
  • Wrinkled MoS2 improved rhodamine B detection, but strain was never measured and the gain was credited to pre-concentration, CT and scattering together (Qiu et al., 2020).
  • Theory predicts that strain changes binding and charge transfer between MoS2 and small molecules such as NO and NH3 (Kou et al., 2014, Fig. 2), using strains up to 10%, far beyond what experiments apply.

Two recent SERS reviews confirm the opening. Jin et al. (2023) write that the effect of pressure or strain on 2D SERS needs to be understood and then cover pressure studies only. The engineering sections of Tang et al. (2024) cover defects, alloys, thickness, heterojunctions and pressure.

The gap this proposal closes

There is no experimental measurement of how controlled in-plane lattice strain of a 2D semiconductor changes charge transfer to an adsorbed dye, on the same flake, with the dye uncompressed and plasmonic effects excluded.

4.Objectives and hypotheses

  1. Build and calibrate a Raman-compatible bending stage that sets strain on monolayer MoS2 reproducibly between 0 and about 0.8%.
  2. Measure how dye Raman enhancement, a CT indicator and MoS2 doping change with measured strain for two dyes and two laser lines.
  3. Establish a protocol that separates strain, doping and electromagnetic contributions at each measurement point.
  4. Compare results with DFT of dye/MoS2 level alignment under strain.
  • H1. Dye enhancement on MoS2 changes monotonically and reversibly with in-plane strain at fixed coverage and doping.
  • H2. The sign of the change depends on which side of the CT resonance the dye and laser sit, so two dyes or two lasers can respond in opposite directions.
  • H3. Built-in growth strain in as-grown flakes accounts for part of the spot-to-spot variation in enhancement.

5.Methodology

5.1Strain as the controlled variable

The same force produces different strain on substrates of different thickness and stiffness, and the flake may slip on the substrate. Reproducibility therefore comes from setting the deflection with a closed-loop actuator and measuring the strain twice at every step: on the substrate with a foil strain gauge, and on the flake with MoS2 Raman and PL. Force from a load cell is logged as a secondary record.

5.2Device design

The stage is a four-point bending fixture (Fig. 3). The actuator pushes two inner rollers up from below while two outer rollers hold the ends from above. The strip bows upward, the top surface (with MoS2 and dye) is in tension, and the region between the inner rollers has uniform strain and an unobstructed view for the objective. Four-point loading is preferred over three-point because small errors in where the laser lands do not change the strain.

Strain on the top surface follows standard beam relations (Fig. 4). For three-point bending the midspan surface strain is ε = 6Dd/L2 (ASTM D790), and for four-point bending with the load span at one third of the support span ε = 4.36Dd/L2 (ASTM D6272), where D is substrate thickness, d is deflection and L is support span. With L = 20 mm, a 250 µm PET or polyimide strip reaches the 0.2–0.8% range of built-in CVD strain within a few millimetres of deflection. These small-deflection estimates are checked against the gauge and Raman at every step.

5.3Samples

  • Route A, direct growth on mica. Our APCVD/LPCVD recipe is run on thin muscovite mica, which survives growth temperatures and can be bent. This avoids transfer. Chae et al. (2017) found that MoS2 couples weakly to mica, so the strain that reaches the flake will be measured on every sample.
  • Route B, transfer to PET or polyimide. MoS2 grown on SiO2/Si is moved with a polystyrene-based transfer, which Bhuyan et al. (2026) found gave fewer defects than PMMA. Starting strain and doping are recorded before any loading.
  • Static reference. As-grown flakes on SiO2/Si are mapped without the stage, so that perimeter-to-centre strain gradients (Luo et al., 2020) give a first strain series at no cost.

Two excitation lines on the same spot (for example 532 and 633 nm, depending on what the lab Raman provides) help separate molecular resonance from CT, and place the dye on different sides of the CT resonance for testing H2.

5.5Measurement loop

At each strain step the sample rests for a fixed time to let polymer creep settle, then the same marked spot is measured (Fig. 5). Strain is stepped up in about five increments to the maximum and back down, and each flake is cycled at least twice after a few preconditioning cycles.

5.6Controls

  • Dye-free MoS2 on the same stage: separates changes in the substrate background from changes in the dye signal.
  • Dye on bare substrate (no MoS2) under the same bending: rules out the substrate itself changing the dye signal.
  • Metal-free samples throughout: keeps electromagnetic enhancement out. Metal particles also strain MoS2 locally (Sun et al., 2014; 2019b).
  • Return-to-zero check after every cycle: the dye and MoS2 peaks must come back within noise, otherwise coverage changed or the flake slipped.
  • Repeated spectra at each step to set the noise floor; low laser power to limit heating and photobleaching.

5.7Data analysis

  • Convert MoS2 Raman positions to strain and carrier density with the E12g/A1g correlation approach used by Chae et al. (2017), using our own PL gauge as a check.
  • Fit normalized dye intensity against measured strain with carrier density as a covariate, using a mixed model with flake as a random effect so that results from several flakes combine properly.
  • Report the slope (change per % strain) with 95% confidence intervals for each dye and laser line, plus loading against unloading residuals as a hysteresis measure.

5.8Optional biaxial extension

A Peltier stage with PID temperature control and MoS2 on polypropylene applies biaxial strain through substrate expansion, set and released in seconds (Frisenda et al., 2017). Biaxial strain gives roughly twice the band shift per % of uniaxial strain, which helps if the bending response turns out small. A rigid SiO2/Si sample heated through the same cycle provides the temperature control.

Size check: with a uniaxial gap gauge near 45–50 meV per % (Conley et al., as cited by Kataria et al., 2017; Frisenda et al., 2017), 0.5% bending strain moves the band by roughly 25 meV, which is small compared with the few hundred meV spanned by alloying. For that reason the dye and laser should be chosen to sit on the steep side of the CT resonance, and the biaxial option is kept in reserve. Measuring this gauge on our own samples is the first checkpoint of the project.

7.Deliverables and timeline

  • A documented, Raman-compatible strain stage (drawings, parts list, control code, calibration data).
  • A dataset of strain, doping and dye response for two dyes and two lasers, with analysis scripts.
  • A first manuscript; working title: Controlled lattice strain tunes dye charge transfer on monolayer MoS2.
  • Inputs for a DFT collaboration on strained dye/MoS2 alignment, building on Kou et al. (2014) and Jing et al. (2014).
